Project Goal
        Our project was to design and build an Air Quality Monitor that provides real-time feedback on indoor air conditions. Many people spend most of their time indoors, often unaware of harmful air pollutants. Poor indoor air can lead to health issues like headaches, dizziness, and long-term respiratory problems. This is especially concerning in places with poor ventilation, such as bathrooms, dorms, or basements. Our goal was to create a simple, affordable, and portable device that alerts users when air quality becomes harmful, so they can take action before symptoms appear.
        This device uses a gas sensor to detect air pollutants, displays real-time status on an LCD1602 screen, and uses LEDs to show safety levels: green for safe, yellow for moderate, and red for harmful and dangerous. When gas levels exceed a dangerous threshold, the system automatically turns on a fan to improve ventilation. The product was built using an Arduino UNO R3 microcontroller, coded in C++.
        Throughout the project, we followed a structured engineering process. We began with defining our goal and conducting a market survey to understand user needs. The survey results helped us prioritize affordability (under $75) and clear real-time feedback. We then developed a Gantt chart to assign tasks and manage our schedule. During the parts ordering phase, we looked for compatible and low-cost components. After receiving the parts, we assembled the system, tested each component individually, and then integrated them. We ran multiple tests using a lighter to simulate poor air conditions, checked LED behavior, and confirmed automatic fan activation. In the end, we concluded the project by reflecting on what worked or didn’t work well and what could be improved in the future.

Survey Results Summary
The majority of participants in our survey reported experiencing unexplained fatigue, lingering odors, or irritation while indoors, highlighting a common concern about unseen air quality issues. Many respondents expressed a strong interest in receiving real-time air quality alerts, indicating a demand for proactive solutions. Additionally, most participants stated they would be willing to pay from $51 to $75 for a simple and effective air quality monitor. Therefore, these insights, supported by our pie chart data, guided our priorities – real-time gas detection, visual alerts, and cost-efficiency.

Testing Description
To evaluate the performance and reliability of our air quality monitor, we conducted a series of hands-on tests under different environmental conditions. Each component, like sensors, LEDs, LCD display, and fan, was tested individually and in combination to verify that the system responds accurately when air quality becomes unsafe. For the gas sensor, we exposed it to butane released from a lighter to simulate dangerous air. For the temperature and humidity, we used ambient room conditions, through one DHT11 sensor, which unfortunately overheated during use. We verified the LED behavior under each threshold level and confirmed that the fan activates appropriately when harmful or dangerous gas levels are detected. The LCD screen was also checked to ensure that it updates and displays sensor readings in real time. Arduino
  



        This section of the code defines a custom 8-row binary array to create a simple character that can be displayed on the LCD screen. It also assigns constant names to specific Arduino pins for the gas sensor, fan, and LEDs, improving code readability and organization. Lastly, the variables sensorValue and gasppm are initialized to 0 and will later store real-time gas readings used to determine whether the air quality is safe or harmful.
  The pinMode() function is used to configure the specified Arduino pins as outputs for the fan and LEDs, allowing them to be controlled by the program The digitalWrite(fanPin, LOW) command ensures the fan starts in the off state. The LCD is initialized with lcd.begin(16, 2) to set up a 16-column, 2-row display. A custom character (a smiley face) is created and stored at position (0,0) in LCD memory using lcd.createChar(0, smile);. The setCursor() and print() functions are used to display the initial welcome message, “Air Quality” on row 0 and “Monitor “ on row 1. The lcd.write(byte(0)) prints the custom smiley character on the screen, followed by a 1000 millisecond delay to let the circuit stabilize, the screen is cleared, and a for loop is used to flash the message “Gas Sensor Ready” twice with a brief pause of 800 milliseconds between each display. This sequence provides a friendly setup message to the user and confirms the sensor system is functional.
  The manageFan() function controls whether the fan is on or off based on the statusNum value. If statusNum is greater than 0, the fan is activated by setting fanPin to HIGH; otherwise, it is turned off with fanPin set to LOW, and “OFF” is printed to the Serial Monitor for feedback. For the ledsOff() function, it turns off all four LEDs (green, yellow, red, and an optional second red) by setting each corresponding pin to LOW.. In the loop() function, the program starts by declaring a static integer fanStatus to track whether the fan should be active. It reads the current analog value from the gas sensor and stores it in sensorValue. This raw value is then mapped to a percentage scale from 0 to 100 using the map() function and stored in gasppm. The comment below the mapping line outlines the air quality interpretation ranges: values under 170 indicate safe air, 201-250 reflect moderate conditions (such as breathing near the sensor), values over 400 suggest harmful air, and values above 700 (such as from alcohol or chemicals) are considered dangerous. Finally, the sensor value is printed to the Serial Monitor to allow real-time tracking and debugging.
  



In this section, a series of if-else statements categorize the air quality based on the sensorValue read from the gas sensor. Each condition first calls ledsOff() to reset all LEDs before setting the status string to reflect the air quality level: “Safe,” “Moderate,” “Harmful,” or “Danger.” It then turns on the corresponding LED color, green for safe, yellow for moderate, red for harmful, and a second red for dangerous levels. For harmful and dangerous levels, the fanStatus is set to 4 or 8, respectively, which corresponds to how long the fan should stay on. After determining the air quality, the LCD is updated: the gas percentage is printed on the first row (lcd.setCursor(0, 0)), and the corresponding status is printed on the second row (lcd.setCursor(0, 1)). At last, the manageFan() function is called with the current fanStatus, and a short delay ensures the system updates smoothly without flickering or overload.

Conclusion
Our project achieved its intended functionality with a high degree of success. The gas sensor accurately detected changes in air quality and triggered appropriate system responses in real time. The LCD screen effectively displayed the gas concentration values, and the LED indicators offered clear, color-coded visual feedback on air safety levels. The fan component operated reliably, activating automatically when thresholds for harmful or dangerous air quality were reached.
Beyond the technical implementation, the group dynamic was positive and productive. Members communicated clearly and consistently, especially when troubleshooting unexpected hardware issues and debugging code. Each member showed strong commitment by actively contributing during both the planning and execution phases. Overall, the combination of functional performance and strong team collaboration was key to the success of the project.
Despite these accomplishments, we also encountered several setbacks. Some components we ordered were not consistent with the ones used in the online tutorials we referenced, which introduced confusion during the assembly process. A significant issue occurred with the temperature and humidity module, which was damaged (melted), likely due to incorrect wiring instructions from unreliable online sources. We also had to exclude the CO₂ sensor from the final design because it was incompatible with our existing hardware setup and available documentation was insufficient to safely implement it.

Recommendation
From these experiences, we developed several technical recommendations for future iterations of the project. It is important to carefully check the specifications and compatibility of all components before purchasing to avoid integration issues. Each sensor or module should be tested separately before being integrated into the full system to simplify the debugging process. Wiring and voltage requirements should always be verified using official data sheets rather than relying solely on informal online tutorials. Additionally, writing clean, modular, and well-commented code can significantly reduce debugging time and allow for easier future updates.
Beyond the technical aspects, we also identified several non-technical strategies that would improve team workflow and project outcomes. Keeping a shared log of tests, bugs, and fixes can help ensure that all team members remain aligned and informed throughout development. When facing difficult challenges, reaching out to instructors or experienced peers can provide valuable guidance and avoid wasted effort. Finally, it is important to remain flexible; if certain goals become unrealistic, the scope of the project should be adjusted rather than risking incomplete or unstable results.
